# A-ANTI-001 — Vision-origin naturalistic Easter

**In short:** This argument supports [[C-A001|Resurrection did not occur]] and opposes [[C-006|Resurrection]]. Easter faith began in grief-driven **visionary experiences**; empty-tomb stories came later as legend. Disciples may have sincerely “seen” Jesus without a reanimated body. Key move: grant appearance *reports*, deny bodily rising.

## Claim targeted

[[C-A001|Resurrection did not occur]] — Jesus did not rise from the dead as classical Christianity claims.

Opposing Pro claim: [[C-006|Resurrection]] — Jesus rose bodily from the dead.


## Thesis

Easter faith originated in grief-driven visionary experiences of Jesus’ followers; empty-tomb narratives are secondary legendary developments and do not historically establish bodily resurrection.

## Argument

Gerd Lüdemann’s historical reconstruction ([[S-027|Lüdemann, Resurrection of Jesus]], popularized pathway in [[S-077|Lüdemann, Resurrection as mourning]]) locates the rise of resurrection belief in psychological and social dynamics after Jesus’ traumatic death: mourning, guilt, and visionary experiences that were interpreted within Jewish symbolic resources. On this model, the disciples’ sincere conviction that they “saw” Jesus is historically real as *experience*, but the experience does not entail a reanimated corpse. Anti therefore grants appearance *reports* while denying Pro’s metaphysical inference.

Empty tomb is treated as later legend. If the earliest recoverable core is visionary proclamation rather than verified tomb inspection, then Habermas/Craig empty-tomb hinges ([[S-048|Craig, Historicity of the Empty Tomb]], [[A-PRO-011|Empty tomb + early creed as resurrection hinges]]) are not load-bearing for the origin of faith. Crossan’s passion and burial skepticism ([[S-078|Crossan, Who Killed Jesus?]]) reinforces that the narrative packaging of death, burial, and tomb discovery is theologically shaped and historically late relative to the first experiences.

Hume’s classic essay on miracles ([[S-023|Hume, Of Miracles]]) supplies the epistemic frame: miracle testimony must overcome the prior improbability of violating natural regularities. Even if naturalistic explanations are individually imperfect, the miracle hypothesis carries a higher burden. Ehrman’s broader exaltation narrative ([[S-025|Ehrman, How Jesus Became God]]) situates resurrection belief inside developing christological interpretation rather than as a brute physical event forcing orthodoxy. Debate media ([[S-082|Horn vs O’Connor resurrection debate]], [[S-086|Early Christianity Was NOTHING Like…]]) illustrate contemporary naturalistic and critical alternatives but are secondary to Lüdemann/Crossan/Hume for scoring.

This argument does not require Jesus-mythicism; it presupposes a historical crucified Jesus ([[A-ANTI-007|Mythicism is fringe — do not overclaim]]). The claim is only that [[C-006|Resurrection]] fails as historical inference. If Pro shows that early creed burial language plus Jerusalem proclamation make occupied-tomb silence historically incredible, this vision-first model is pressured.
