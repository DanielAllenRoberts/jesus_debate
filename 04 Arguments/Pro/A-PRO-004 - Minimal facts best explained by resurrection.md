# A-PRO-004 — Minimal facts best explained by resurrection

**In short:** This is Pro’s flagship Easter case for [[C-006|Resurrection]], against [[C-A001|Resurrection did not occur]]. **Minimal facts** = a short list of data many critical scholars already grant (crucifixion, appearance experiences, early proclamation, key conversions). Pro says bodily resurrection explains that package better than stacked naturalistic alternatives.

## Claim targeted

[[C-006|Resurrection]] — Jesus rose bodily from the dead.

Opposing Anti claim: [[C-A001|Resurrection did not occur]] — Jesus did not rise from the dead as classical Christianity claims.


## Thesis

A small set of historically well-attested data about Jesus’ death and the rise of Easter faith is best explained, under ordinary historical method, by Jesus’ bodily resurrection rather than by purely naturalistic alternatives.

## Argument

The argument does not begin from Christian dogma. It begins from data that a wide range of critical scholars already treat as historically secure, then asks which explanation best accounts for that data without special pleading. Gary Habermas’s “minimal facts” framing in [[S-047|Habermas, Minimal Facts approach]] and the Habermas–Licona popularization in [[S-004|Habermas & Licona, Case for the Resurrection]] list roughly: Jesus’ death by crucifixion; experiences of the disciples in which they believed they saw the risen Jesus; the early public proclamation of the resurrection; and the transformation of key early figures (including, on many lists, James and Paul). Michael Licona’s larger historiographical treatment in [[S-005|Licona, Resurrection of Jesus (historiography)]] presses the same point: once those data are granted, competing hypotheses must be weighed by explanatory scope, power, and plausibility.

Crucifixion under Pilate is among the firmest points in historical Jesus research and is treated separately in [[A-PRO-010|Crucifixion under Pilate among firmest HJ facts]] with [[S-008|Tacitus, Annals 15.44]] and Josephan material in [[S-009|Josephus, Antiquities (Jesus & James)]]. The load-bearing hinge for this argument is therefore not “did Jesus die?” but “what followed?” Habermas’s essay on experiences of the risen Jesus ([[S-037|Habermas, Experiences of the Risen Jesus]]) argues that appearance traditions stand near the foundation of the earliest proclamation. The pre-Pauline creed embedded in 1 Corinthians 15, discussed across [[S-037|Habermas, Experiences of the Risen Jesus]], [[S-004|Habermas & Licona, Case for the Resurrection]], and [[S-003|Wright, Resurrection of the Son of God]], is especially important: it places death, burial, resurrection, and appearances in a tradition Paul says he “received,” commonly dated within a few years of the crucifixion. That timing compresses the window available for slow legendary accretion of the *appearance* core, even if Gospel narratives continue to develop later.

N. T. Wright’s [[S-003|Wright, Resurrection of the Son of God]] adds a second constraint: Second Temple Jewish resurrection expectations typically pointed to a general eschatological raising, not the isolated raising of a crucified messianic claimant mid-history. The early Christian claim that *this* Jesus had already been raised, and that this event grounded messianic identity, is historically anomalous on Wright’s reading and therefore needs a historically adequate cause. Licona ([[S-005|Licona, Resurrection of Jesus (historiography)]]) and the Craig–Ehrman debate materials ([[S-051|Craig–Ehrman resurrection debate]]) then compare candidate causes: hallucination/grief visions, legendary embossing, stolen body, wrong tomb, and bodily resurrection. Pro’s claim is comparative, not absolute: resurrection is argued as the *best* explanation of the conceded data set, not as a premise smuggled in before the data.

Lee Strobel’s interview volume ([[S-006|Strobel, Case for Christ]]) is secondary popularization of expert testimony and should not be treated as primary scholarship; it is cited here only as a widely influential presentation of the same hinges. The decisive scholarly load remains Habermas/Licona method ([[S-047|Habermas, Minimal Facts approach]], [[S-004|Habermas & Licona, Case for the Resurrection]], [[S-005|Licona, Resurrection of Jesus (historiography)]]), Wright’s contextual analysis ([[S-003|Wright, Resurrection of the Son of God]]), and the early creed/appearance traditions ([[S-037|Habermas, Experiences of the Risen Jesus]]).

If naturalistic alternatives can match the early creed’s timing, the disciples’ conviction, and the rise of resurrection proclamation without ad hoc stacking, then this argument fails. If they cannot without multiplying weaker hypotheses, then [[C-006|Resurrection]] is historically supported relative to [[C-A001|Resurrection did not occur]].

## Symmetry notes

Anti may use the same comparative historical method, the same early sources, and the same demand for explanatory scope. Pro may not treat scholarly majority counts as proof if Anti produces equally cited minority reconstructions that explain the same data.
