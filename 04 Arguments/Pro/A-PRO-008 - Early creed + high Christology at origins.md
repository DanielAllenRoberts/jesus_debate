# A-PRO-008 — Early creed + high Christology at origins

**In short:** This argument supports [[C-003|Jesus Son of God died for sins]] against “divinity was invented centuries later.” It points to very early creeds and devotion treating Jesus as exalted Lord already in the first Christian generation. **High Christology** = beliefs that place Jesus extremely high (divine/exalted), not merely a wise rabbi.

## Claim targeted

[[C-003|Jesus Son of God died for sins]] — Jesus is the divine Son of God, and his death saves people from sin.

Opposing Anti claim: [[C-A003|Nicene Christianity later construction]] — Nicene-style orthodox Christianity was largely built later, not simply taught by the historical Jesus.


## Thesis

Exalted devotion to Jesus and early creedal formulas placing him at the center of salvation arise within the first Christian generation, which undercuts the claim that divine Son / atonement identity is merely a late Nicene political construction.

## Argument

Larry Hurtado’s early high Christology program ([[S-039|Hurtado, Early High Christology / Lord Jesus Christ]]) argues that practices of Jesus-devotion — invoking Jesus, singing to him, treating him as rightful recipient of honor normally reserved for God — appear remarkably early in the Christian movement. This is a historical claim about cultic practice and belief patterns, not a claim that Nicaea’s philosophical vocabulary was already present in the 30s CE. Pro carefully distinguishes **early exalted status** from **later creedal metaphysics**.

The 1 Corinthians 15 creed materials treated in [[S-037|Habermas, Experiences of the Risen Jesus]] and resurrection discussions ([[S-004|Habermas & Licona, Case for the Resurrection]], [[S-003|Wright, Resurrection of the Son of God]]) likewise place death “for our sins,” burial, resurrection, and appearances inside a received tradition. That already couples Jesus’ death to salvific meaning and exalted status before the fourth-century councils. Dunn’s broader historical Jesus / early Christianity work ([[S-038|Dunn, Jesus Remembered]]) is more cautious than maximal high-Christology readings, but still locates significant christological development inside the first generations rather than only at Nicaea.

Anti’s opposing package ([[A-ANTI-004|Christology developed after Jesus]], [[A-ANTI-005|Orthodoxy as later winner]]) uses Ehrman’s exaltation-to-incarnation arc ([[S-025|Ehrman, How Jesus Became God]]), Bauer’s orthodoxy-and-heresy thesis ([[S-029|Bauer, Orthodoxy and Heresy]]), Pagels on pluriform early Christianity ([[S-028|Pagels, Gnostic Gospels]]), and the historical fact of Nicaea/Constantinople ([[S-089|First Council of Nicaea]], [[S-090|First Council of Constantinople / Creed]]). Pro grants: (1) Christian diversity existed; (2) councils settled contested formulas; (3) incarnation metaphysics developed. What Pro denies is the stronger inference that therefore [[C-003|Jesus Son of God died for sins]]’s core — Jesus as divine Son whose death deals with sin — is a *late invention* with no early root. Early creed + early devotion are offered as the root.

[[A-PRO-005|Resurrection vindicates divine identity and atonement]] then argues that if [[C-006|Resurrection]] holds, resurrection uniquely vindicates those early identity claims. The present argument does not require resurrection to be proven; it only requires that high claims about Jesus are early enough that “Nicene construction alone” is historically inadequate as a full origin story for [[C-003|Jesus Son of God died for sins]].
