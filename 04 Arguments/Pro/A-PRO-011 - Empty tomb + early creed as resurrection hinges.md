# A-PRO-011 — Empty tomb + early creed as resurrection hinges

**In short:** This argument supports [[C-006|Resurrection]]. Two hinges work together: (1) a very early creed about death–burial–resurrection–appearances, and (2) historical arguments for an empty tomb. Together they squeeze “visions only + late legend” reconstructions. A **creed** here means a short fixed tradition Paul says he “received,” not a fourth-century council text.

## Claim targeted

[[C-006|Resurrection]] — Jesus rose bodily from the dead.

Opposing Anti claim: [[C-A001|Resurrection did not occur]] — Jesus did not rise from the dead as classical Christianity claims.


## Thesis

Two independent hinges — a very early death–burial–resurrection–appearances tradition and historically defensible empty-tomb argumentation — jointly constrain naturalistic Easter reconstructions and support bodily resurrection as the better explanation.

## Argument

The first hinge is chronological. Paul’s citation of the creed in 1 Corinthians 15 (treated in [[S-037|Habermas, Experiences of the Risen Jesus]], [[S-004|Habermas & Licona, Case for the Resurrection]], and [[S-003|Wright, Resurrection of the Son of God]]) places core Easter proclamation inside living memory of the events. “Buried” in that creed is not a late Gospel flourish; it is already part of the tradition Paul says he received. That matters because vision-only models often treat burial and empty tomb as optional later legend. If burial is early, then “appearances” are not free-floating religious experiences detached from a known death and known burial location.

The second hinge is the empty tomb. William Lane Craig’s peer-reviewed case for the historicity of the empty tomb ([[S-048|Craig, Historicity of the Empty Tomb]]) and Wright’s broader treatment ([[S-003|Wright, Resurrection of the Son of God]]) argue that tomb emptiness is multiply attested in the passion tradition, coheres with Jewish burial practice, and is presupposed by early Jewish polemic that the body had been taken. Pro does not claim the empty tomb is as widely granted as the crucifixion; [[S-048|Craig, Historicity of the Empty Tomb]] itself treats it as more contested. The claim is narrower: once burial is early and Jerusalem proclamation begins soon after, an occupied tomb known to opponents would have been a simple rebuttal.

Crossan’s skepticism about passion and burial tradition ([[S-078|Crossan, Who Killed Jesus?]]), used by Anti in [[A-ANTI-001|Vision-origin naturalistic Easter]] and [[A-ANTI-006|Gospel distance allows legendary embossing]], is therefore load-bearing for the opposing side. If burial itself is late fiction, the empty-tomb hinge collapses. Pro’s reply is that the creed’s “buried” clause, Paul, and the Gospel passion cores still require a historically adequate burial story earlier than a post-70 free composition. Licona ([[S-005|Licona, Resurrection of Jesus (historiography)]]) and Habermas–Licona ([[S-004|Habermas & Licona, Case for the Resurrection]]) treat empty tomb plus appearances as jointly harder for hallucination theories: visions do not by themselves explain why a known tomb would be proclaimed empty in Jerusalem.

This argument therefore pairs with [[A-PRO-004|Minimal facts resurrection case]]: minimal facts without empty tomb may still favor resurrection for some scholars; with empty tomb, purely psychological Easter models must also explain absence of a body. If Anti successfully severs burial from the early creed or shows empty-tomb arguments fail under critical method, this hinge weakens without automatically falsifying every appearance-based case.
